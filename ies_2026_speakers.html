From Restaurant Floor to Robot Fleet: Building Bear Robotics

Abstract

In 2017, after years as a software engineer at Google, John Ha ran a Korean restaurant in Silicon Valley as a side project — and discovered firsthand how physically and mentally demanding restaurant service work is, and how hard that labor is to staff. That experience became the founding insight behind Bear Robotics: autonomous service robots that handle the repetitive, exhausting parts of hospitality work so people can focus on food and guests. This talk traces Bear Robotics' path from a single prototype tested in its founder's own restaurant to a fleet of AI-driven robots operating across North America, Europe, and Asia, and its recent transition into logistics and industrial deployments backed by a major strategic investment from LG Electronics. It's a story about spotting a real, unglamorous problem, building the technology to solve it, and scaling a hardware-and-AI company through a genuine strategic partnership.

Biography

Jungwoo "John" Ha is the Founder and CEO of Bear Robotics, the maker of Servi, one of the world's most widely deployed autonomous service robots. Since founding the company in 2017, he has grown Bear Robotics from a restaurant-tested prototype into a global robotics company whose robots operate in restaurants, healthcare facilities, retail, and logistics settings across the US, Korea, Japan, and beyond — a growth trajectory that culminated in a major strategic investment and majority stake from LG Electronics. Before founding Bear Robotics, he spent nearly two decades as a software engineer, including as a Senior Software Engineer and Technical Lead at Google, and served as an Adjunct Professor at the Korea Advanced Institute of Science and Technology (KAIST). He holds a B.S. in Computer Science and Engineering from Seoul National University and a Ph.D. in Computer Science from the University of Texas at Austin.

Talk

Cannabis from Prohibited Drug to Therapeutic Discovery

Abstract

Cannabis sativa L. produces more than 150 cannabinoids, among which Δ9-tetrahydrocannabinol (THC) and cannabidiol (CBD) are the most abundant and biologically active compounds. In recent years, cannabis has gained substantial scientific and medical attention due to the therapeutic potential of cannabinoids, particularly CBD. CBD-based therapeutics such as Epidiolex® have demonstrated efficacy in treatment-resistant epileptic seizures, contributing to the expanding medical acceptance of cannabis-derived therapeutics. The biological effects of cannabinoids are mediated through the endocannabinoid system (ECS), a neuromodulatory network composed of endogenous cannabinoids, cannabinoid receptors (CB1 and CB2), and metabolic enzymes that regulate neuronal excitability, synaptic plasticity, pain perception, emotional regulation, and neuroprotection. Notably, cannabinoid receptors are largely absent in brainstem respiratory centers, distinguishing cannabinoids from opioids that can induce fatal respiratory depression. Emerging evidence suggests cannabinoids possess anticonvulsant, neuroprotective, anti-inflammatory, and anxiolytic properties with therapeutic implications for epilepsy, Parkinson's disease, Alzheimer's disease, chronic pain, and psychiatric disorders. The recent Schedule III reclassification of cannabis in the United States further expands opportunities for cannabinoid-based neuroscience research, clinical translation, and next-generation drug discovery.

Biography

Sanghyuck Park, PhD, is the Associate Director of Research at the Institute of Cannabis Research (ICR) at Colorado State University Pueblo and the CEO of Green Medicine USA, a biotechnology company focused on developing CBD-derived therapeutics in collaboration with the Catholic University of Korea College of Medicine. Dr. Park received his PhD in Plant, Soil and Microbial Sciences from Michigan State University and previously served as a Research Molecular Biologist with the USDA Agricultural Research Service (USDA-ARS) and a Postdoctoral Associate at the University of Arizona. His research focuses on cannabis genomics, cultivation, cannabinoid science, plant physiology, and biotechnology. He has published more than 41 peer-reviewed articles and holds U.S. patents related to cannabidiol technologies. His contributions have been recognized with the ACS ElSohly Award from the American Chemical Society's Cannabis Chemistry Subdivision and multiple Colorado State University Demo Day Innovation Awards. Dr. Park leads international research collaborations, chairs the ICR Cannabis Cultivation and Plant Science Webinar Series, and mentors graduate and undergraduate researchers.

Talk

Before the Killer App: Where Quantum Actually Is—and Why the Money Isn’t Waiting

Abstract

Quantum headlines often force founders into one of two positions: expect a near-term revolution or dismiss the field. This talk offers a third approach — judge what uncertainty today's work removes. A recent experiment placed a small quantum adapter inside a classical large-language-model workflow, showing that real quantum hardware could participate in the loop, but not that it delivered computational advantage. That distinction captures the market today. Public investment is buying learning, manufacturing capability, scientific access, and independent evidence rather than immediate productivity. Commerce asks, "Can we build it?" DOE asks, "What should it solve?" DARPA asks, "Is it worth it?" Semiconductor and AI history explain why this matters: those industries scaled when specialized layers connected through repeatable processes, predictable interfaces, shared infrastructure, and real demand. Quantum has credible pieces, but those connections are not yet settled. Semiconductor history is therefore a map, not a clock. For AI and technology founders, the opportunity is not predicting the winning hardware. It is solving bottlenecks — measurement, control, interfaces, validation, and customer workflow — that remain valuable across multiple technical paths. Bet on a problem, not a hardware winner.

Biography

Saesun Kim, PhD, is a Member of Technical Staff at Sygaldry Technologies, a Y Combinator-backed startup building quantum-accelerated AI servers, which has raised $139M from investors including Breakthrough Energy Ventures and Initialized Capital. His work spans the full stack of quantum systems engineering — from superconducting-qubit control and automated calibration to quantum sensing. Previously, he was a Technical Lead at Keysight Technologies, where he built automated calibration workflows for scalable quantum computers, and a postdoctoral researcher at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, where he developed atomic quantum sensors for space applications. He holds a PhD in physics from the University of Oklahoma, specializing in quantum optics and squeezed light. He was named to the "Quantum 100" by UNESCO's International Year of Quantum (IYQ 2025).

Building Hard Tech Is Hard

Abstract

The world's most pressing industrial challenges in energy, advanced compute, robotics, health tech, and manufacturing demand hard tech breakthroughs because software alone is not enough. Yet hard tech startups face a uniquely difficult path. Complex systems are slow to iterate, capital-intensive to build, and unforgiving of missteps.
